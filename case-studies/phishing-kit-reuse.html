Case Study

Credential-Harvest Kit Reused Across Three Brands

Timeframe Q2 2024
Role Tier 2 Analyst · Lead investigator
Attack type Phishing · credential harvesting
Outcome 3 takedowns · IOC bundle published

Same kit signature, same hosting fingerprint, three different brand impersonations linked back to one operator cluster. The pattern was in the kit, not the IOCs.

The Alert

A low-confidence phishing alert hit the queue via a brand-monitoring correlation rule tied to newly registered domains. The landing page mimicked a financial-services client's login portal but lacked obvious credential-collection indicators, which blocked automated escalation.

What caught my attention wasn't the domain itself; it was hosting overlap and structural similarity to two separate alerts I'd processed earlier that month targeting unrelated brands. Different domains, but the page behavior, resource-loading patterns, and TLS configuration looked too familiar to be coincidence. I stopped treating it as a standalone impersonation and opened it as a campaign-level investigation.

What I Saw

From: "Support <support@secure-update-portal[.]com>"
Reply-To: helpdesk@mailer-relay[.]net
Return-Path: bounce+[redacted]@mailer-relay[.]net
Authentication-Results: spf=fail dkim=none dmarc=fail

URL: hxxps://account-review-center[.]com/auth/verify/session
Hosting ASN: AS[redacted], VPS provider previously flagged for phishing abuse
TLS: Self-issued certificate reused across multiple sibling domains

The sender anomalies alone weren't conclusive; low-quality phishing routinely produces failed authentication headers, and that's just noise. What made this different was the landing-page construction. The resource-bundle names, JavaScript loading sequence, and /auth/verify/{session} pathing closely mirrored two prior investigations involving different client brands. That consistency pointed to kit reuse by a single operator, not three separate actors independently building similar pages.

How I Investigated

  1. Pivoted on passive DNS to map infrastructure scope. The newly observed domain shared registrar patterns and hosting allocation with multiple sibling domains registered within a narrow timeframe. They impersonated different organizations but resolved through overlapping infrastructure with nearly identical deployment timing, suggesting centralized operator management, not isolated attempts. I started here because infrastructure clustering would determine whether this was a one-off or a campaign worth escalating.
  2. Pulled TLS certificate metadata and extracted kit resources in sandbox. The HTML structure, obfuscated JavaScript sequence, and static asset naming conventions matched artifacts from two earlier credential-harvesting cases. The strongest link was the same resource-bundle hash appearing across all three: 
    sha256: 9f3c…b21a
Classification: credential_harvester_v3
Matched: [Campaign A], [Campaign B], [Campaign C]
    This moved the investigation from IOC-level triage to campaign-level attribution.
  3. Controlled interaction with the phishing flow to map exfiltration behavior. Outbound authentication requests were redirected through a relay endpoint consistent with prior kit behavior, same URI structure, same backend response formatting. This confirmed operator reuse, not just kit similarity.
  4. Mapped to MITRE ATT&CK: T1566.002 (Spearphishing Link), T1056.003 (Web Portal Capture), T1071.001 (C2 over HTTPS).
  5. Consolidated into an operator-cluster assessment and escalated. Domains, hosting indicators, TLS overlaps, kit fingerprints, and exfiltration patterns packaged as a single cluster for internal escalation.

What It Turned Into

Three coordinated takedown requests filed against the identified phishing infrastructure. Two domains disabled within 24 hours; the third suspended shortly after following registrar escalation. The IOC package, phishing domains, hosting infrastructure, certificate fingerprints, landing-page hashes, email-routing indicators, and behavioral notes, was distributed internally for correlation and detection enrichment.

The investigation also surfaced additional related infrastructure added to proactive monitoring, which caught later campaign variants earlier in their lifecycle.

What I'd Do Differently

The connection between the three campaigns only clicked because I'd personally triaged the earlier two alerts. That's not a process, that's luck. After this case, I pushed for a kit-fingerprinting step in the standard triage workflow: hash the resource bundle on every phishing alert and check it against prior cases before treating anything as standalone. Would have caught the overlap on day one instead of day three.
